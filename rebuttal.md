- Re. Weakness 1: "even if there aren't barren plateaus, that still doesn't necessarily tell.." This is absolutely true. As with classical ML, it is, in general, an NP-hard problem to minimize the training loss even if BP is not present. However, in QML, we need to deal with a much more intrinsic problem in training before even thinking of local optimum: there have various theoretical contributions showing that for supervised learning tasks, QML models that avoid BP (hence non-vanishing gradient) can also be classically simulated, meaning that these models, even if can be trained successfully, can be replaced equivalently by for instance, classical NN with poly. resources. Hence, the fundamental question one must ask in QML is: **"Is my quantum model free of BP, and moreover, does it mean my quantum model can be replaced by classical computation, and hence no need for a quantum computer at all?"** This is arguably the biggest difference in QML research and the classical counterpart, posing a fundamental bottleneck of quantum models -- dichotomy between classical simulability and trainability (free of BP), whose investigation culminates in [Does provable absence of barren plateaus imply classical simulability?](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-63099-6) by Marco Cerezo. It has shown that, for the most supervised learning QML, free of BP indeed implies classical simulability. This is the major reason why in QML, trainability commonly refers to the non-presence of BP, instead of looking into the time complexity of finding near-optimum solutions, as in classical ML. Hence, our work is of strong theoretical importance for QML since we show that for a generative modeling task, it is possible to construct a quantum model that is **both trainable/free-of-BP and spits out a distribution family that is NOT classically simulable**.
- Re. Weakness 2: "why do we want to do gradient descent over IQP circuits?" We thank the reviewer for raising this important question. We agree that classical sampling hardness alone does not imply a useful inductive bias, improved sample quality, or an advantage on any concrete machine-learning task. In particular, an IQP circuit may be difficult to simulate for reasons unrelated to the statistical structure of real-world data. Our work does not claim that IQP-QCBMs currently outperform classical generative models. Determining whether their accessible distribution families provide a useful inductive bias for high-dimensional discrete generative modeling remains an important open problem.
  
  The purpose of our analysis is to address a logically prior question: can the two necessary conditions for a meaningful quantum generative model—trainability and classical non-simulability—coexist at all? Neither condition is sufficient for useful quantum advantage, but failure of either is already disqualifying. If the model is efficiently classically simulable, then its sampling capability cannot provide a computational quantum advantage. Conversely, if every classically non-simulable architecture has exponentially vanishing gradients, then the model cannot be trained at scale by gradient-based methods. Recent theoretical work in supervised QML has highlighted precisely this apparent tension: architectures with trainable gradients are frequently classically simulable, whereas sufficiently expressive or non-simulable architectures tend to exhibit barren plateaus, which has been considered a fundamental roadblock for QML by many. The only thing we really do for "practical QML" is attempt to kill it by spoofing or non-trainability.

  Our results at least show that this is not the case for IQP in generative settings, and that indeed it makes a lot of sense to see what these IQP models can do. And this model is exciting because: 1) it does not get killed by non-trainability or classical simulability arguments and 2) it can, to a point, be tested on real-world cases, which is being done but not a part of this theory paper. And we find this really game-changing.

Question 1: The major reason is the existing theoretical works/framework of BP, e.g., the DLA approach by [Ragone et. al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-49909-3) and the adjoint representation approach by [Fontana et. al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-49910-w), can not be applied to IQP-circuits: these two landmark works in BP applied to general variational circuits only if the initial state $\rho$ and the observable $O$ have a **non-zero projection onto the dynamic Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ of the circuit**. For IQP, the DLA is $\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{i Z^{s_j}\}$, where $Z^{s_j}$ are the Pauli-Z generators of IQP-circuits, while the observable is Pauli-X, such that, for every Pauli-X operator $O=X^a$ with nonzero support string $a$, $$\Tr(X^aZ^{s_{j}}) = 0\implies X^a \not\in \mathfrak{g}.$$Hence, IQP circuits are beyond the scope/capability of previous SOTA theoretical works, and we have to develop a new theory for the IQP-circuits, independent from the previous works on BP. 
- Re. Question 2: **The restriction is that our exact characterization (critical-rank expression in Thm. 3.3) concerns uniform measure**. Trainability is formally defined relative to a reference measure on parameter space, so uniformity is not an intrinsic part of the barren-plateau definition. Proposition 3.1 already permits any reference measure symmetric about zero; uniformity is specifically required for the exact critical-rank expressions in Thm. 3.3. We choose the product-uniform distribution because the IQP parameter space is a torus, and this distribution is precisely its normalized Haar measure thereon. Since the IQP circuit map is a group homomorphism from the parameter space to $SU(N)$, it also induces Haar measure on the reachable commuting subgroup. It therefore provides a canonical, translation-invariant notion of a typical unstructured initialization and enables the exact character-orthogonality calculation underlying our critical-rank formula. This is why we call it most natural and common choice. Nevertheless, the exponential-versus-polynomial scaling classification is unchanged under initialization measures whose density relative to Haar is bounded above and below by polynomial factors. We will clarify this scope in the manuscript.
- Re. Question 3: Yes. For the sparse IQP distribution we studied, we took the average-case hardness assumption of its zero-output amplitude. We shall add the precise assumption to the appendix of the paper when revising it. More precisely, we took the following construction from [BMS17](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2017-04-25-8/): Fix a sufficiently large constant $\gamma>0$. Let $\mathcal{D}_n=\mathcal{D}_n^\gamma$ be the sparse IQP distribution on $n$ qubits. An instance $C\sim \mathcal{D}_n$ is generated as follows.
	- For every unordered pair $(i,j)$, independently include a two-qubit diagonal interaction with probability $p=\gamma\frac{\log n}{n}$.
	- Conditional on inclusion, choose the two-qubit phase uniformly from. $\{\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,\omega^k): k\in\{0,1,2,3\}\}, \omega=i$. 
	- Independently, choose each one-qubit phase uniformly from $\{\operatorname{diag}(1,\zeta^k): k\in\{0,\ldots,7\}\}, \zeta=e^{\pi i/4}$.
  Thus the underlying graph is $G(n,\gamma\log n/n)$, with additional random edge and vertex labels. With high probability such circuits have $\mathcal{O}(n\log n)$ two-qubit gates. For such an instance, define the corresponding sparse Ising partition function $$ Z_C(\zeta)=
  \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}
  \zeta^{\sum_{i<j} w_{ij}x_ix_j+\sum_i v_ix_i},
  \qquad \zeta=e^{\pi i/4},$$where the weights $w_{ij}$ and $v_i$ encode the two-qubit and one-qubit IQP phases. Up to the usual $2^{-n}$ normalization, $Z_C(\zeta)$ is the zero-output IQP amplitude. We then take the following hardness assumption for $Z_C(\zeta)$: 
	- There is a universal constant $c_0>0$ such that approximating $|Z_C(\zeta)|^2$ to relative error $1/4+o(1)$ on an arbitrary $c_0$ fraction of instances $C\sim\mathcal{D}_n$ is \#P-hard.

- Re. Weakness 1: "why the DLA-based approach of Ragone et al. [2024] does not directly apply to IQP-QCBMs..". With due respect, the DLA-based approach does **NOT** apply here. The main result, Theorem 1 assume the Lie algebra is semisimple, i.e., $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus \mathfrak{g}_{2}\oplus\cdots$, where each $\mathfrak{g}_i$ is simple. In Eq. (9) of [Ragone et. al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-49909-3) , the following assumption must hold: **the numerator needs a non-zero $\mathfrak{g}$-purity term for both the initial state $\rho$ and the observable $O$** for at least one simple Lie sub-algebra $\mathfrak{g}_{i}$. The $\mathfrak{g}$-purity of an Hermitian $H$, denoted by $P_{\mathfrak{g}}(H)$ is defined in Eq. (7), which is essentially the purity of $H$, restricted to the complexification of $\mathfrak{g}$, or equivalently, given an orthonormal basis $(B_{j})_{j}$ of the algebra, we need to compute the squared projection of $H$ onto each basis, i.e., $|\Tr(B_{j}^\dagger H)|^2$. Now, for IQP circuits, the DLA is abelian $\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{i Z^{s_j}\}$, where $Z^{s_j}$ are the Pauli-Z generators of IQP-circuits and $Z^{s_j}$ are also an orthogonal basis of $\mathfrak{g}$. The observable $O$ is Pauli-X, such that, for every Pauli-X operator $O=X^a$ with nonzero support string $a$, $$\Tr(Z^{s_{j}}X^a) = 0\implies P_{\mathfrak{g}}(X^a) = 0 \implies \operatorname{Var}_{\theta}[\ell_{\theta}(\rho, X^a)] = 0\,,$$which is apparently false (variance cannot be zero, which implies a constant loss). Hence, IQP circuits are examples beyond the scope/capability of previous SOTA theoretical works, for which we have to develop a new theory for the IQP-circuits, independent from the previous works on BP.

- Re. Weakness 3: "The claim that the sparse ER architecture is both trainable and classically non-simulable should be stated with caution."  Yes, indeed, we should be more specific with the trainability only for low-Hamming-weight frequencies. However, we emphasize that in practice, this theoretical restriction does not constrict the usefulness of the model since modeling and matching the high-Hamming-weight frequencies requires inevitably much more computation resources that are intractable in practice. Take the classical moment-matching framework/models, a $k$-order moment or correlator is determined by the Fourier coefficients of the distribution at frequencies up to Hamming-weight $k$. In practice, researchers and practitioners only attempted to match up to the third-order moment, beyond which the time/space-complexity becomes impractical for large dimensions. Hence, we argue that, being able to match the Fourier spectrum of the target distribution at low-Hamming-weight frequencies is theoretical incomplete indeed, yet in practice, the computational method employed by the SOTA generative models.

- Re. Question 2: The time complexity is not dependent on the restriction on the frequency weights. Assume we have $D(n)\in\operatorname{poly}(n)$ generators in the IQP circuit. The time complexity to compute the MMD value up to error $\varepsilon$ with at least $1-\delta$ probability is $$\Omega\left(\frac{D(n)}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\,.$$We show an informal proof of the complexity to compute the MMD or its gradient as follows. Let $\mathcal A_k=\{a\in\mathbb F_2^n:1\le |a|\le k\}$ and define $W_k=\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_k}\Lambda(a),\widetilde\Lambda_k(a)=\frac{\Lambda(a)}{W_k}$. The truncated MMD is $L_{k}(\theta)=W_k\,\mathbb E_{A\sim\widetilde\Lambda_k}\!\left[\bigl(C_p(A)-C_\theta(A)\bigr)^2\right].$ The IQP characteristic function has the Monte-Carlo representation (see the paper of [Joseph Bowles](https://arxiv.org/search/quant-ph?searchtype=author&query=Bowles,+J)) $C_\theta(a)=\mathbb E_{Z\sim U(\mathbb F_2^n)}[f_{\theta,a}(Z)], f_{\theta,a}(z)=\cos L_{\theta,a}(z)$, where $L_{\theta,a}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{D(n)}\theta_j(-1)^{s_j\cdot z}\left(1-(-1)^{s_j\cdot a}\right), |f_{\theta,a}(z)|\le1$. Since $C_p(a)=\mathbb E_{X\sim p}[\chi_a(X)]$ with $\chi_a(x)=(-1)^{a\cdot x}$, define $R_{\theta,a}(X,Z)=\chi_a(X)-f_{\theta,a}(Z),|R_{\theta,a}(X,Z)|\le2$. Then $\mathbb E[R_{\theta,a}(X,Z)]=C_p(a)-C_\theta(a)$. To estimate its square without bias, draw $$A_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\widetilde\Lambda_k,\qquad X_t,X_t'\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}p,\qquad Z_t,Z_t'\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}U(\mathbb F_2^n),$$with all replicas independent, and set $$\widehat L_k(\theta)=\frac{W_k}{N}\sum_{t=1}^N R_{\theta,A_t}(X_t,Z_t)R_{\theta,A_t}(X_t',Z_t').$$Then $\mathbb E[\widehat L_k(\theta)]=L_k(\theta)$. Since each summand lies in $[-4W_k,4W_k]$, Hoeffding's inequality gives $$\Pr\left(|\widehat L_k-L_k|\ge\varepsilon\right)\le2\exp\!\left(-\frac{N\varepsilon^2}{32W_k^2}\right) \le 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{N\varepsilon^2}{32}\right)\,,$$since $W_k^2 \le 1$. Therefore, it is sufficient to take $N\ge\frac{32}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{2}{\delta}$ MC shots (sample complexity). Thus the truncated MMD can be estimated without enumerating the $|\mathcal A_k|$ frequencies. As for the time complexity, consider the time complexity of each $f_{\theta,a}$ function is $\Theta(D(n))$, and therefore the overall time complexity is $\Omega(\frac{D(n)}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac{2}{\delta})$.
- Re. Question 3: We defined the MMD metric (in its spectral form) in Proposition 2.2 at line 108. We agree that we should also add the original definition of MMD in the paper. MMD is good loss for IQP-type circuit because 
	- It can be **efficiently estimated with a classical computer**, meaning that we can train the IQP-QCBM model entirely with classical computing resource, despite that sampling bitstring/generation tasks requires us to deploy the model on a quantum computer, which is the unusual "train classically, deploy quantum" separation.
	- MMD is a weighted 2-norm of spectral difference of the generator and the target distribution (see Proposition 2.2): $$\operatorname{MMD}^2(p,q_{\theta}) = \sum_{a\in \{0,1\}^n}\Lambda(a) (C_{p}(a) - C_{\theta}(a))^2\,,$$in which the Fourier components of the generator $C_\theta(a) = \Tr(X^a U_{\theta}\rho  U_{\theta}^{\dagger})$  are Pauli-X string expectation values, for which the special IQP structure provides classical Monte Carlo estimators. This is what permits the unusual "train classically, deploy quantum" separation. MMD is therefore not an arbitrary generic loss: its RKHS features are exactly the Fourier/parity quantities naturally exposed by the model.
	- If equipped with a characteristic kernel, convergence in the MMD value implied convergence in distribution, hence the MMD metric is consistent with the typical notation of convergence in distributional learning.
